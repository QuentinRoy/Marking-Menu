PROTOTYPE — measure-and-solve label placement (#267)

Question: is a measure-then-grow-the-radius loop an acceptable way to place label plates around the wedge ring, and what does it actually cost and guarantee? This page renders real DOM plates (real fonts, real CSS box model, the content-hug + clamp rule from #276), measures them with getBoundingClientRect, and runs two solvers side by side: the design canvas's 2px linear scan, and a closed-form direct solve. Compare their radius, iteration count and timing; stress-test with more/longer items; and check determinism across repeated measurement and simulated browser zoom.

Neither solver above checks two things a plate must also respect: not covering a different item's connector, and not sitting on top of the ring itself. A radius that clears every pair of plates can still let a wide plate sit across a neighbor's connector line, or dip inside the ring's own selection area. The "Long labels" and "Manual angles" presets show both happening; the third solver, "connector-safe", raises the floor to clear the ring under every item, then finds the exact radius windows where a connector would be covered and pushes past them, still without scanning. See the Solve panel.

Four separate clearances feed the solve, and they are easy to mix up because two of them share a word: the plate-plate gaps set how far two different plates must stay from each other (horizontal and vertical are separate because the plates are wider than they are tall, so a pair stacked vertically needs much less slack than a pair side by side); the plate-ring clearance sets how far every plate stays from the ring, and did not exist as its own knob until now: the earlier version placed plates with zero clearance, right up against the ring's edge. Try "Plate–plate horizontal gap" alone on the default preset: it does nothing up to its slider maximum, because for this menu's label shapes the vertical gap (or the ring clearance) is always the tighter constraint first.

Exploratory, not part of the recommended answer above: the "combined" row takes near-anchoring and lets each plate also slide tangentially, within whatever bound still keeps some point of it on its own ray, picking the radius and every plate's slide together. Sliding moves a plate off its ray, which changes its distance from the origin, so the center radius is re-solved for the slid position and near-anchoring's zero spread survives the slide rather than being traded away for it. Every nudge is an exact derivative of whichever constraint is violated rather than a numerical approximation, so a pass costs the same as the collision checks themselves; the re-solve is closed form except at angles where a box corner, not an edge, is nearest the origin.

One thing that pass needs on top: the relaxation stops at the first feasible arrangement, with no preference among them, so it used to keep whatever slide it happened to land on. On a 12-item menu an 8px spread in label width was enough to leave one item slid 39px and its mirror image 24px the other way, which reads as lopsided even though both are feasible. Shrinking every slide back toward zero afterward, as far as it will go while the layout stays feasible, removes that: mirror-image items now settle at equal and opposite offsets, at the same height.

Holding every nearest point on one exact circle is rigid: a single crowded item can force the whole menu outward when a little slack for that item alone would do. "Ring-distance tolerance" turns the circle into a band, letting an item's nearest point sit anywhere in [r, r + tolerance]. Slack is a fallback rather than a first move, so the tolerant search is a superset of the strict one and can never do worse; a first attempt that pushed items out as part of the same relaxation did do worse, by 6% on the 12-item menu and 12% on the adversarial one, because the radial pushes fought the tangential ones. Worth knowing: the smallest radius is not the smallest menu. Past a modest tolerance the radius keeps dropping while the outer extent grows again, because items fling outward. On the 12-item menu a 20px tolerance cut the extent by 32%, while 150px cut the radius far more but the extent only 20%.

Also exploratory: "near-anchored" is a purely radial alternative to centering every plate on the shared circle, no tangential slide at all. It anchors each plate's nearest point to the ring on the shared circle instead, which is why a wide item's near edge sits much closer to the ring than a short item's does under center-anchoring: the gap between center and near edge is just that item's own half-width or half-height, and plates here are far wider than they are tall. Near-anchoring makes every item equidistant from the ring by construction, exactly zero spread, at the cost of no longer being a free win: a wide item now needs a much larger center radius than a short one to reach the same near distance, so label width variance can inflate the overall radius, sometimes past the center-anchored answer, sometimes under it. It reuses minRingClearRadius directly, pointed at the shared circle instead of the physical ring.
